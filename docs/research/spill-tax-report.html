The Spill Tax

Eighteen GPU-layer counts — from 3 all the way to 41, the model's ceiling — four context sizes, one 35B mixture-of-experts model on an 8 GB card. About 170 model loads across four sweeps. Far enough up the ladder to find where generation speed stops improving, dense enough around that point to see its shape, and enough to kill the threshold theory the earlier, shorter ladder suggested.

GPURX 6600 XT, 8176 MiB
BackendVulkan, b10956
CPURyzen 5 5600X
ModelQwen3.6‑35B‑A3B

1 · The ladder

Eighteen rungs, dense where the structure is, all the way to the ceiling

The ladder is deliberately uneven, because the interesting structure is not evenly spaced. Every integer from 3 to 8 resolves the boundary between configurations that fit in VRAM and ones that don't, which earlier work placed somewhere in there. 14, then +3 all the way to 41 (40 transformer layers plus the output pseudo-layer — llama.cpp's own ceiling for this model) covers the whole oversubscribed range, up to asking the card to hold 16.8 GB of weights in 8 GB of VRAM. 16 and 18 were added last, either side of ngl 17, once the first pass up the ladder put the decode peak there.

Rungs 3–8, 14, 16 and 18 were measured in three independent runs, so those carry a real p50 and spread. The rest of the high rungs (17, 20–41) were measured once — three passes at the top of the ladder would have run 20+ hours and, at these levels of oversubscription, carries a documented risk of driver-timeout cascades on this card. The table marks which is which; nothing mixes n=3 and n=1 silently.

Two points at the very top could not be measured at all: at ngl 41, contexts of 8k and 16k never finished loading inside the 120-second startup budget the application itself allows. That's reported as a failure, not dropped.

2 · Method

One choreography, about 170 loads

Each load is a full llama-server restart at that exact (ngl, ctx), driven by worker/src/runtimeBench.ts's own executeCurvePoint unmodified — the same N1 request choreography the production Benchmark page uses: warm_discard to prime the pipeline (uncounted), cold_timed at n_predict = 1 against the full prompt (PP and TTFT), then three warm_repeat requests generating 64 tokens each with cache_prompt = true (TG). Context size is set to the tested value exactly; the prompt fills it to within 128 tokens of the ceiling.

Four sweeps produced the numbers below. A 3-pass speed sweep over ngl 3–14 (28 points × 3 = 84 loads, the whole ladder walked three times rather than repeating each point back to back, so no two measurements of a point sit close together in time). A memory sweep over the same 28 points, sampling GPU memory once a second throughout each load. And a high-ngl sweep over 17–41 (35 load attempts), capturing speed and memory together in one pass with a 15-minute per-request timeout, because the production completion path has no timeout of its own and these configurations can stall for a very long time. Finally, a summit sweep over ngl 16 and 18 (24 loads), three runs each, interleaved — run 1 of 16, run 1 of 18, run 2 of 16, and so on — so neither rung's runs cluster in time. That sweep also recorded llama-server --list-devices immediately before every load, to put llama.cpp's own idea of free VRAM next to what the card actually held.

p90 and p99 are the same number here, and that's disclosed rather than hidden. Where a point has 3 samples, the 90th and 99th percentile both resolve to the maximum of those 3 — there is no third value to distinguish them, so every "p90/p99" figure is really "the highest of 3 runs," and p50 is the middle one. Where a point has 1 sample (ngl 17 and above), p50 and p90/p99 are the same single measurement and no spread exists at all. Real percentiles would need an order of magnitude more samples per point; three replicates catch whether a point is stable, not what its tail looks like.

GPU core clock and hotspot temperature are still not in this report. LibreHardwareMonitor's Remote Web Server was not running elevated this session either.

3 · Results

A cliff, a summit, and a ceiling

Every chart below is interactive. Click a layer count in any legend to isolate that rung — it stays highlighted and labelled while the rest dim, and the selection applies to every chart on the page at once, so you can follow one configuration from prompt speed through to its memory footprint. Click again to release it, or pick several to compare. Hovering any point gives its exact value.

Layer count is encoded as a ramp, not as separate colours: low counts light, high counts deep. It's an ordered quantity, and with sixteen rungs a ramp reads as the ladder it actually is.

PP — prompt processing rate

tok/s, from llama-server's own timings.prompt_n / timings.prompt_ms. Median of 3 loads for ngl 3–16 and 18; single measurement for 17 and 20–41. Axis starts at zero.

Prefill is binary. There are two flat bands and nothing in between: ngl 3–6 run at 100–147 tok/s, and ngl 7–41 run at 19–40 tok/s. The cliff sits between 6 and 7 and never recovers — but it also never gets meaningfully worse. Going from ngl 7 to ngl 41 multiplies the spilled memory by 27×, from 366 MiB to 10,012 MiB, and prefill does not care: ngl 41 at ctx 2k turns in 39.3 tok/s, among the fastest readings in the entire slow band, while holding 10 GB of weights in system RAM.

This kills the threshold theory outright. The earlier, shorter ladder suggested a spill-size threshold somewhere between 160 and 240 MiB, with small spills costing little and large ones costing 3–5×. The full ladder falsifies it in both directions. ngl 5 spills 158 MiB and is fast (100 tok/s). ngl 8 spills 141 MiB — less — and is slow (26 tok/s). Two configurations with all but identical spill, sitting on opposite sides of a 4× performance cliff. Whatever separates them, it is not how many megabytes ended up in system RAM.

TG — token generation rate

tok/s, mean of each load's 3 warm_repeat samples. Median of 3 loads for ngl 3–16 and 18; single measurement for 17 and 20–41. Axis starts at zero.

Decode has an optimum, and it is not at the top. Generation climbs steadily with layer count, tops out around ngl 16–17, and then falls away for the rest of the ladder. At ctx 1k, in order: 9.70, 10.04, 10.15, 10.56, 10.81, 11.13 (ngl 3–8), 12.16 (14), 12.80 (16), 13.07 (17), 12.46 (18), 12.47 (20), then 11.49, 10.81, 10.43, 9.86, 8.97, 8.58, 8.47 (41). An inverted U, the same shape at every context.

The top is a plateau, not a point. Bracketing ngl 17 with three runs each of 16 and 18 shows how flat it is. ngl 17 has the highest reading at ctx 1k, 2k and 16k; ngl 16 has it at 8k (10.87 against 10.40); and ngl 16's own best single run at 1k, 13.10 tok/s, edges past ngl 17's only one. Past the plateau the decline is real but shallow at first — ngl 18 and 20 sit within a few hundredths of each other at 1k and 8k — and only becomes steep and unambiguous from about ngl 23 up.

That summit matters practically: full offload is the worst decode setting on this card, 35% slower than the plateau and slower even than ngl 3, which puts almost nothing on the GPU. And the best settings are ones that spill — ngl 16 and 17 each push roughly 860–890 MiB into system RAM and are still the fastest configurations measured. Up to a point, buying more GPU-resident layers is worth paying some spill for; past it, the spill costs more than the layers return.

An earlier reading of the shorter ladder called this relationship monotonic — "more layers on GPU always means faster decode, regardless of spill." That was an artifact of the ladder stopping at 14. It stops being true three rungs later.

Measured spill

MiB llama.cpp put on the GPU minus this process's dedicated VRAM peak. Thin vertical ticks show the min–max across 3 loads where the passes disagreed.

Above the cliff the spill grows almost perfectly linearly with layer count — roughly 380 MiB per additional layer, which is simply this model's per-layer weight size going somewhere other than VRAM. It reaches 10 GB at full offload, on a card with 8 GB total.

Two departures from that line are worth noting. ngl 8 at ctx 16k goes clean (−91 MiB) after spilling 141–166 MiB at the three smaller contexts — the only rung that crosses back over zero. And ngl 14 is the one configuration in the entire 3-pass sweep that failed to reproduce: pass 1 measured 129, 134, 219 and 26 MiB at its four contexts; passes 2 and 3 both measured 243, 248, 268 and 139 MiB, identical to each other and different from pass 1 every time. Every other rung gave bit-identical spill across all three passes. See §4.

PP vs. measured spill — every measured point

Each point is one configuration. The vertical gap between the two bands is the whole finding; the horizontal spread within the lower band is noise around it.

Laid out against spill directly, prefill shows no dose-response at all. The fast band sits at the far left, clustered near zero spill. The slow band stretches from 134 MiB to 10 GB along the x-axis and stays resolutely flat in y — a horizontal smear, not a slope. If spilled megabytes were what cost prefill its speed, this chart would fall away to the right. It doesn't.

The honest conclusion is narrower than the one this report previously drew: something that happens between ngl 6 and ngl 7 costs prefill a factor of four, and the amount of memory displaced is not it. Establishing what it actually is would need per-buffer allocation tracing — which specific tensors land in shared memory, not how many bytes — and that is past what Windows' counters and llama.cpp's own logs can show.

Memory footprint: what llama.cpp claims vs. what's actually held

Five more readings, sampled once per second for the full duration of every load. Claimed is llama.cpp's own self-reported GPU allocation (model + KV + compute buffers) from its startup log. Per‑process is this specific llama-server process's own reading (Windows GPU Process Memory). Global is the whole adapter — every process on the machine combined, sitting a fairly constant 1.1–1.5 GiB above the per-process figure, which is this desktop's own background GPU footprint (compositor, browser, LibreHardwareMonitor) riding along under every measurement in this report.

Together these four charts show the ceiling doing its work. Claimed VRAM climbs in a straight line the whole length of the ladder, from 1.6 GB at ngl 3 to 16.8 GB at full offload. Held VRAM does not: it tracks claimed exactly up to about ngl 14, then flattens hard at ~6.1–6.8 GB and stays there for the entire rest of the ladder — ngl 17 and ngl 41 hold the same amount of real VRAM as each other. Everything claimed past that point goes to shared memory instead, essentially one-for-one: per-process shared rises from 1.5 GB at ngl 17 to 10.5 GB at ngl 41.

llama.cpp's own free-memory figure doesn't predict any of this. The dashed line on the claimed, held and global charts is what llama-server --list-devices reported immediately before each of the 24 summit-sweep loads: 7,378 MiB free, identical every single time — including before the one run that spilled 380 MiB more than its repeats. Held VRAM never reaches that line at any rung; its highest reading anywhere in the report is 6,833 MiB, about 550 MiB short. And spilling starts far below it: ngl 5 spills 158 MiB while claiming only 2,408 MiB, less than a third of what llama.cpp reports as free. A fit check built on that number would pass configurations that measurably spill.

The arithmetic closes cleanly. Across every rung, held VRAM plus shared memory exceeds claimed by a near-constant ~500 MiB — the driver's own non-buffer overhead, which this project's earlier calibration work on this same card independently pinned at 430–480 MiB. Two separate measurement paths, years apart in the codebase, agreeing on the same constant.

4 · Caveats

What the raw numbers don't show at face value

Most high rungs are single measurements — including the one at the top. ngl 17 and 20–41 were each measured once, so they carry no spread and a single unlucky load would not show as one. That matters most for ngl 17 itself: it sits between two rungs with three runs each, and which of 16 and 17 is actually higher is not resolved — ngl 16's three runs at 1k spanned 11.95 to 13.10 tok/s, a range wide enough to contain ngl 17's lone 13.07. The existence of a plateau near 16–17, with decline on both sides, holds at every context. Pinning its exact top would need three runs of ngl 17 too.

TTFT still plateaus at ~30 s and is not first-token latency. 50 of the 70 measured points report TTFT in a narrow band around 30 s regardless of how long the load actually ran — and those loads ran anywhere from 34 s to 866 s. The 17 that finished inside 30 s report TTFT exactly equal to their end-to-end time, as they should. The pattern is consistent with a periodic keep-alive frame on llama-server's SSE stream arriving long before the real first token. PP is unaffected: it comes from llama-server's own prompt_ms timing, not from the client-observed stream.

Every warm_repeat request in every sweep is flagged cache_evicted — 3 per load across all 170 successful loads, none of which hit the prefix cache. TG is still computed from the decode-only window after the first token, and lands in the same band this application's own probe path measures through an entirely different request path, so it is treated as trustworthy despite the flag.

The first run of a sweep places memory differently from the runs after it. This has now happened at three rungs in two separate sweeps. In the 3-pass sweep, ngl 14's spill was lower in pass 1 than in passes 2 and 3 at all four contexts, while every other rung reproduced bit-for-bit. In the summit sweep, run 1 of both ngl 16 and ngl 18 spilled more than their later runs — at ctx 1k, 1,271 against 887 and 887 MiB, and 1,652 against 1,524 and 1,524 — while runs 2 and 3 matched each other almost exactly. The direction isn't consistent (lower for 14, higher for 16 and 18); what's consistent is that the first loads of a session are the ones that differ. Later runs followed a stretch of continuous heavy GPU load; the first ones followed none. This is the same shape of effect the earlier PP/GPU Investigation on this box documented for a different model — allocator state accumulating over a long session of heavy GPU use — here surfacing as a shift in which layers spilled rather than in raw speed.

GPU core clock and hotspot temperature are absent from this report entirely. They are only available on this machine through LibreHardwareMonitor's Remote Web Server, which was not running elevated for any of the four sweeps. Every sample came back null. Nothing has been estimated in their place, which does mean thermal throttling cannot be ruled out as a contributor to anything here — though the earlier investigation on this card tested that directly and found clock held near maximum boost throughout.

5 · Bottom line

What the full ladder establishes

  • Prefill is binary, and the split is not about spill size. ngl 3–6 run at 100–147 tok/s; ngl 7–41 run at 19–40 tok/s. Between those bands is a 4× cliff with nothing in it. Crucially, ngl 5 spills 158 MiB and lands in the fast band while ngl 8 spills less (141 MiB) and lands in the slow one — so the amount of displaced memory cannot be what causes the penalty.
  • Past the cliff, more spill costs nothing further. From ngl 7 to ngl 41 the spill grows 27×, to 10 GB on an 8 GB card, and prefill stays flat — full offload at ctx 2k is among the fastest readings in the slow band. This falsifies the spill-size threshold this report previously proposed on the shorter ladder.
  • Decode tops out on a plateau at ngl 16–17, not at full offload. TG rises to about 13 tok/s there, then falls to 8.47 at ngl 41. Three runs each of 16 and 18 show the top is flat: 17 leads at three contexts, 16 at the fourth, and which is truly higher isn't resolved. Full offload is the worst decode configuration tested, slower even than ngl 3.
  • The best decode settings are ones that spill. ngl 16 and 17 each push roughly 860–890 MiB into system RAM and are still the fastest configurations measured. Some spill buys more than it costs; roughly 2 GB of it stops doing so.
  • Held VRAM saturates at ~6.1–6.8 GB and every claimed byte past that goes to shared memory one-for-one. ngl 17 and ngl 41 hold the same real VRAM as each other while their claims differ by 9.5 GB. Held + shared exceeds claimed by a near-constant ~500 MiB of driver overhead — matching this project's own independent calibration of 430–480 MiB on this card.
  • --list-devices free memory is not a usable fit check. llama.cpp reported 7,378 MiB free before all 24 loads where it was recorded, never varying. Held VRAM never got within 550 MiB of it, and spilling began at a claim of just 2,408 MiB.
  • Full offload stops being loadable at all past ctx 2k. At ngl 41, contexts of 8k and 16k never became ready inside the 120-second startup budget the application allows.
  • Not established: what actually changes between ngl 6 and ngl 7 to cost prefill a factor of four, given it demonstrably isn't spill volume — answering that needs per-buffer allocation tracing, not counters. Also open: whether ngl 16 or 17 is the true top (17 needs three runs of its own), why the plateau sits there at all, and whether any of this generalises past Vulkan on this particular AMD card.
